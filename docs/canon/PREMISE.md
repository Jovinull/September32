# Premise

**Status: PROVISIONAL** — the shape is settled; the specifics are not.

## One paragraph

A student arrives at a small residential school and is assigned to a job nobody
wanted: digitising the archive. Photographs, yearbooks, tapes, letters, lost
property, attendance ledgers, the student paper, decades of administrative
sediment. The job is tedious, the people doing it are interesting, and for a
while that is the whole game. Then documents start turning up dated **September
32** — a day that does not exist. Some of them appear to prove that people were
there.

## Tagline

> September has thirty days. Your school has records of one more.

## What the player does

Catalogues, talks, chooses how to spend a limited number of hours a day, and
gradually assembles an account of something that does not admit of a clean
account. The archive is the spine: it is a job, a social space, a source of
evidence, and eventually a problem.

## What the game is not

**Not a mystery with a human story attached.** `CANON`. The human story is the
game. A player who investigates lightly and mostly gets to know people should
have a complete experience — see `THEMES.md`.

**Not route-based.** `CANON`. There is no "Iris route". Relationships are
multi-dimensional and simultaneous, and the ending reflects the life the
player's decisions produced, not which menu branch they entered.

**Not horror from the first hour.** `CANON`. The escalation is
`normal → odd → unsettling → impossible → consequences`, and the first phase is
meant to be genuinely pleasant. See `docs/narrative-design/PACING.md`.

**Not meta-horror in the DDLC sense.** `CANON`. No file manipulation, no fake
crashes, no character addressing the player early. What crosses between
playthroughs is the *player's* knowledge, not the protagonist's. See
`docs/narrative-design/META.md`.

## Two principles the whole design serves

> **Before you break the routine, make the player like the routine.**

> **The mystery must change human relationships, and human relationships must
> change how the mystery is discovered.**

The second is the harder one and the more important. A romance system and an
investigation system running in parallel would be two games in one package.
They have to interfere: who trusts you determines what you are shown, and what
you know determines what you can ask.
