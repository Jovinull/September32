# Themes

**Status: PROVISIONAL**

Themes are not a message. They are what the game keeps returning to, and what
makes one scene feel like it belongs with another. When a scene is not working,
the usual reason is that it is not about any of these.

## What the game is about

### Records outlive the people who made them

Somebody wrote fourteen names in a ledger and went home. Decades later the
handwriting is all that is left of an afternoon. The archive is full of attention
that outlasted the person paying it — sports days, somebody's aunt, a corrected
photocopy of a typed list — and most of it is about nothing at all. That is not
sad, exactly. It is the texture the game lives in.

### Being believed, and believing

The mystery is an engine for the human question: what does it cost to tell
someone something they will find difficult to accept, and what does it cost to be
the person who does not accept it?

Every character's relationship to evidence is also their relationship to other
people. Theo re-checks the ledger because checking is how he cares. June refuses
the story because accepting it would cost her something the player learns about
later.

### The specific against the general

Forty faces, no names. The game is repeatedly interested in the difference
between "a class photograph" and *this* photograph, between a person and a line
in a list. Iris photographs things before cataloguing them because cataloguing
turns a thing into a line, and she cannot stand it.

### Ordinary life is not the prelude

The slice-of-life is not throat-clearing before the horror. It is what the horror
is *about*. A player who spends their time on people and barely investigates
should reach a real ending, not a lesser one.

## Tonal commitments

**Warmth first.** The default register is affectionate. The characters like each
other. The jokes land. If the first hour is not pleasant, the rest does not work.

**Understatement.** The prose does not tell the player how to feel, and it never
tells them something is wrong. It reports, and lets them notice.

**No cruelty for texture.** Bad things happen; the game does not enjoy them.

**The unexplained stays unexplained where that is truer.** Not everything gets an
answer, and the ones that do not should feel deliberate rather than unfinished —
which requires that the ones that *do* get answers are genuinely answered.

## What the game is not about

- Whether the supernatural is real. It is about what people do with evidence.
- Institutional villainy. Ms. Ellery is not a conspiracy; she is a person with a
  filing system and a job.
- The protagonist being special. They got the job because nobody else wanted it.
