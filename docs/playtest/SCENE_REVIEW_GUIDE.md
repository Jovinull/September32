# Scene review guide

The thirteen scenes from
[`HUMAN_ACT1_REVIEW.md`](../production/HUMAN_ACT1_REVIEW.md), turned into things
that can be learned from a playtest rather than from a reading.

**This document is internal.** Do not send it to a tester and do not use its
wording in a question. Every entry has a *what not to tell the tester* line, and
in most cases it is the whole point of the entry.

The difference from `HUMAN_ACT1_REVIEW.md` matters. That document asks somebody
to *read* the scene with the intent in front of them, which is a useful thing
and a different thing: a reader who knows what a scene is for will usually find
it there. This one asks how to find out whether a person who was just playing
noticed anything at all.

Each entry names the hypothesis it feeds, so a wave summary can be assembled
from the questionnaires without going scene by scene.

---

## 1. `mara_is_wrong` — H12

**Question.** Did the tester come away thinking Mara is sometimes wrong, and
can they say why?

**What we want to learn.** Whether `confidence != correctness` survives contact
with a reader. It is the property the whole mystery structure rests on: it is
what lets a wrong explanation be offered honestly, by somebody who believes it.
If Mara reads as simply reliable, every ordinary explanation in the act reads as
the author's own view.

**How it arrives.** Deep questionnaire B8, then cross-referenced against the
report: did this run actually contain the scene? A tester who never saw it and
still reads her that way is much stronger evidence than one who did.

**Do not tell the tester.** That Mara is wrong in that scene. That she is wrong
anywhere. That reliability is a design axis.

---

## 2. `june_is_not_fine` — H14, H07

**Question.** What does the tester think June wants during the week?

**What we want to learn.** Whether the deflection registered as a person having
a bad afternoon, or as nothing. This is the scene most at risk of being about
nothing — it is deliberately about nothing, and the line between "understated"
and "empty" runs exactly through it.

**How it arrives.** B6, plus whether June appears in B1's spontaneous recall.
Recall is the better signal: a scene about nothing that somebody remembers is
working.

**Do not tell the tester.** That she is not fine. That the phone matters.

---

## 3. `the_common_room` (anchor) — H01, H07

**Question.** Does the group scene appear in the first three remembered moments?

**What we want to learn.** Whether four people in a room, none of it about the
player, reads as a cast having a life — or as a scene the player is not in. It
is the act's clearest claim that these people exist independently, and every run
plays it.

**How it arrives.** B1, and F3 (an ordinary scene they especially liked). Since
every run sees it, absence from every tester's recall is itself a finding.

**Do not tell the tester.** That it was rewritten. That the mug does anything.

---

## 4. `theo_the_quiz` — H13

**Question.** What does Theo care about besides being right?

**What we want to learn.** Whether the notebook landed, and — the specific
worry — whether it landed *too hard*. This is the most sentimental scene in the
game. An answer that repeats the subtext back verbatim is not a success; it may
mean the scene said the thing it was supposed to leave unsaid.

**How it arrives.** B5. Read the *phrasing*, not just the content: "he's lonely"
and "he keeps score for people who stopped turning up" are different results.

**Do not tell the tester.** Anything about the notebook, the names, or
loneliness.

---

## 5. `iris_the_second_box` — H04

**Question.** Did any character seem to treat the tester differently because of
something they had done earlier?

**What we want to learn.** Whether `stance()` is legible from inside a single
playthrough. A player sees one version of this scene and has nothing to compare
it against, which is the hard part — reactivity that is only visible across two
runs is not reactivity the first run can feel.

**How it arrives.** C1, cross-referenced with the report: which stance did this
tester actually reach? A `wary` run whose tester noticed nothing is a different
result from a `comfortable` run whose tester noticed nothing.

**Do not tell the tester.** That relationships are tracked. That scenes vary.

---

## 6. `annexe_two_of_them` — H05

**Question.** Which choice did they think about longest, and did any feel
obviously correct?

**What we want to learn.** Whether both positions read as right. The scene's
whole claim is that neither Noah nor Iris is wrong; if testers finish knowing
whom they were supposed to side with, it has failed. If they name this as the
choice they thought about longest, it has worked.

**How it arrives.** C2 and C5. The failure signature is naming it in C5.

**Do not tell the tester.** That it is meant to be balanced.

---

## 7. `the_date` (anchor) — H08, H10, H19

**Question.** What did they most want answered when it ended, and would they
continue?

**What we want to learn.** Whether the ending is a hook or a stop. Also — and
this is the expensive one — whether the placeholder photograph blunted it. The
class photograph is `P0` in the art plan and needs a commission; this wave is
what decides whether it needs one *before* Act 2 rather than during.

**How it arrives.** D5, D6, D7, and G3. G3 is asked once, late, and plainly.

**Do not tell the tester.** That the two documents are meant to have no single
ordinary explanation. That the photograph is the object the premise turns on.

---

## 8. `courtyard_small_talk` — H04, F4

**Question.** Did anything repeat more than they wanted it to?

**What we want to learn.** This is the most-played scene in the game, around
five times a run, and it now reads which characters the player has got close to.
Whether the third reading still works is not something the writer can judge.

**How it arrives.** F4, and the report's own play counts. If F4 names it, the
rewrite did not go far enough; if F4 names something else, that something else
is the new problem.

**Do not tell the tester.** That it repeats by design, or that it varies.

---

## 9. `mara_the_long_way` — H03, H07

**Question.** Does it appear in spontaneous recall?

**What we want to learn.** Whether the dog is memorable or whether the writing
is admiring itself. Bill is one of the seven candidate memorable moments and the
one with the least plot attached, which makes him the cleanest test of whether
the ordinary material works on its own.

**How it arrives.** B1 and F3.

**Do not tell the tester.** Anything. Especially not the dog.

---

## 10. `june_reads_it_flat` — H09

**Question.** Before the final discovery, what explanations were they
considering?

**What we want to learn.** Whether canon rule R5 is actually honoured: a player
must never feel stupid for agreeing that the undated column is somebody's
Tuesday. If no tester lists a mundane explanation, the ordinary reading is not
surviving contact, and the act has no tension — only waiting.

**How it arrives.** D3, free text, never a multiple choice. A list of options
would supply the very reading we are trying to find out whether they reached.
Also D4: did anybody's explanation convince them, and whose.

**Do not tell the tester.** That there is a right answer. That there is a rule.

---

## 11. `ellery_and_the_form` — H19, art priority

**Question.** Did any missing image make a scene hard to understand?

**What we want to learn.** Ms. Ellery is played off-sprite, as a voice and a
folder, because no sprite exists. **Whether that reads as a stylistic choice or
as an absence decides whether the character-art brief is P1 or P0.** It is the
one art question this wave can settle cheaply.

**How it arrives.** G1 and G2. If a tester names her without being asked about
her, that is a strong P0 signal.

**Do not tell the tester.** That she has no sprite, or that it was not deliberate.

---

## 12. `noah_takes_a_call` — H11

**Question.** What do they think Noah is hiding, and which moment made them
think it?

**What we want to learn.** The scene exists to keep alive the reading that Noah
is avoiding something *unrelated*. The risk, written down before any tester saw
it, is that a phone call he does not want to take reads as a clue however
ordinary the prose is — in which case the scene achieves the opposite of its
purpose and should be cut.

**The decision rule, fixed in advance.** If most testers who saw the scene name
it as their reason for suspecting Noah, cut it. Do not soften it: softening
produces a different scene and destroys the comparison. This was written down
before the wave on purpose, because a rule invented after the results is not a
rule.

**How it arrives.** B9, asked only after Act 1, never naming the scene. The
second half — *which moment* — is the measurement.

**Do not tell the tester.** That the scene is uncertain, that it was flagged, or
that anything about Noah is in question.

---

## 13. `theo_admits_it`, third branch — H05

**Question.** Which choice, if any, did they regret?

**What we want to learn.** The only available cruelty in the act, and the only
branch that lowers a trust axis. A single cruelty in a whole act is either very
effective or conspicuous. Regret is the good outcome; *not taking it because it
looked like a trap* is the bad one.

**How it arrives.** C3, and C5 for the trap reading. The report says whether
they took it, which turns a vague answer into a comparison.

**Do not tell the tester.** That it is the only one.

---

## Reading the results

**Do not fix a scene on one tester's reaction.** With five to eight people, one
answer is an anecdote. What to act on is convergence — and even then, see §70:
five people agreeing can mean the thing is true, or that five people share a
taste, or that one scene misled all five in the same way.

**Absence is data.** A scene that every run plays and no tester remembers has
told you something, and it is not "write more scenes".

**A scene nobody saw is not evidence about that scene.** Check the reports
before drawing a conclusion. Several of these are reached by fewer than three
runs in four.
